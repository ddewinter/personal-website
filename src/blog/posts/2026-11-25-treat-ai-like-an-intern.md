I say "treat Claude like an intern" so often when I teach people to use AI that I joke I say it 500 times a session.

It's the most useful frame I know. An intern is smart, fast, and eager. They also know nothing about you, your customers, or what good looks like on your team. Hand them a vague request and you get a confident, generic answer back.

AI works the same way. The difference is what you hand it.

## Give it what an intern would need

Here's an example from a training session. We asked for a report on a set of business metrics and got back exactly what you'd expect: accurate, tidy, and generic. Then we added five pieces of context:

1. Who the audience is
2. Lead with a recommendation
3. What success looks like
4. What this report should not decide
5. Cite the sources

Same data, same model. The second version was something an executive could act on.

## Every answer is a coin flip

Here's the part people skip. AI doesn't look up an answer. It generates one, and the next time you ask, it might generate a different one. I describe every response as flipping a coin 10,000 times: the same prompt can land differently on every run.

Put another way, the fact that it worked once doesn't mean it will work next time. Checking the output is your job, every time.

## How I check the work

These are the checks I actually use:

- **Ask for the plan first.** Before it writes anything, have it tell you how it will approach the task. A bad plan is much faster to fix than a bad draft.
- **Let it interview you.** "Ask me one question at a time" pulls out the context I forgot to give it.
- **Ask where it was on the fence.** After it answers, I ask it to cite its sources and tell me where it was least sure. That's where I look first.
- **Get a second reviewer.** The one that wrote it shouldn't grade it. Anything that goes to another person gets looked at by a separate AI reviewer that never saw the original conversation. I've also learned to tell that reviewer to check the evidence itself, not the author's summary of it.

Checking is the part I'm still worst at. It's easy to skim an answer that looks good and move on.

## Match your effort to the risk

Not every task needs every check. I think about it in three levels:

- **Low risk.** Routine work that follows a template. Hand it over freely.
- **Medium risk.** Work that matters but follows a known process. Write the process down, have AI follow it, and review what comes back.
- **High risk.** One-off, strategic work. Bring your own thinking first. If you don't, you'll get the average of everything the model has ever read.

AI wants to please you. Left alone, it will polish your first idea instead of questioning it. So I tell it to poke holes in my thinking and not to anchor on what I've told it.

## You still own it

My biggest worry about AI isn't that it makes mistakes. It's that people stop thinking critically because the answer sounds right.

The rule I give everyone: if you can't answer questions about it, don't send it. The intern can write the first draft. Your name is still on it.
